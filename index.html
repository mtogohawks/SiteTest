What is Wheel Throwing?

Wheel throwing is the art of shaping clay on a spinning potter's wheel. Using the centrifugal force of the wheel combined with the pressure of your hands, you pull a lump of clay upward into bowls, cylinders, mugs, vases, and more — all perfectly symmetrical, all made by hand.

It's one of the most iconic and meditative crafts in the world. The learning curve is real, but the moment a wobbly lump of clay transforms into a centered, rising vessel is unlike anything else.

Why Learn Wheel Throwing?

  • Create symmetrical, functional pieces with your hands
  • Deep focus and flow — a moving meditation
  • Endless variety: mugs, bowls, vases, plates, and more
  • Build tactile skill and spatial awareness
  • Join a craft practiced for over 6,000 years

Tools & Setup

Wheel throwing requires a handful of essential tools — here's what you need.

●

The Pottery Wheel

Electric wheels are the standard for beginners — consistent speed, easy to control. Kick wheels use a foot-powered flywheel and are quieter, but require more coordination. Most studios have electric wheels.

●

Clay

Stoneware is ideal for wheel throwing — it's plastic (flexible), forgiving, and durable when fired. Avoid very coarse or groggy clays until you're more experienced. Start with 1–2 lb balls.

●

Water & Sponge

Water is your lubricant on the wheel. Keep a bucket nearby and use a natural sponge to keep the clay moist. Too little water causes friction and tears; too much weakens the walls.

●

Wire Clay Cutter

A thin twisted wire used to cut finished pieces off the wheel head. Pull it flat against the bat while the wheel is still — a clean, quick motion releases the piece.

●

Wooden Rib & Metal Rib

Ribs are used to smooth and compress the outside walls of a thrown piece. A wooden rib refines curves gently; a metal rib scrapes and thins walls more aggressively.

●

Needle Tool & Loop Tool

The needle tool levels uneven rims and scores clay for joining. The loop trimming tool is used after throwing to carve away excess clay and shape the foot ring.

The Throwing Process

Every thrown piece follows these core steps — master them one at a time.

01

Wedging

Before touching the wheel, wedge your clay — knead it firmly on a flat surface to remove air bubbles and make the consistency uniform. Air pockets can cause pieces to crack or explode in the kiln. Ram's head or spiral wedging are the two main methods.

02

Centering

Slam the wedged clay firmly onto the center of the wheel head. With the wheel spinning fast, use the heels of both hands to push inward and downward until the clay runs true — no wobble. Centering is the most critical skill in throwing; everything else depends on it.

03

Opening

With the wheel still spinning fast, press your dominant thumb or fingers straight down into the center of the clay to create the floor of your vessel. Stop about ½ inch from the wheel head. Then pull outward to set the width of the base. Keep your elbows braced against your body for control.

04

Pulling Up the Walls

Place fingers inside and outside the wall simultaneously. Squeeze gently and pull upward in one smooth motion from the base to the rim. Repeat 3–5 times, adding a little water each pass, to thin and raise the walls. Slow the wheel slightly as walls grow taller.

05

Shaping

Once the walls are at the desired height, use inside pressure and a rib on the outside to push the form outward (for a bowl) or inward (to collar a neck). Work slowly — small adjustments go a long way. This is where your piece takes its final character.

06

Trimming the Rim & Cutting Off

Use a needle tool to level an uneven rim by touching the tip to the wall while the wheel spins and tearing off the excess. Then compress the rim with a wet finger or chamois. Finally, wire the piece off the bat with a slow, flat pull and set it aside to stiffen.

07

Trimming the Foot (Leather Hard)

Once the piece is leather hard (firm but not dry), flip it upside down onto the wheel and use a loop tool to carve the foot ring and clean up the base. A well-trimmed foot makes the piece feel light and finished. Re-center the inverted piece carefully before trimming.

Firing & Glazing

From Wheel to Finished Piece

  1. Greenware — Your freshly thrown, unfired piece. Let it dry completely and evenly (days to weeks) before firing to avoid cracking.
  2. Bisque Firing — The first kiln firing (~1800°F / 982°C) hardens the clay and burns off organics, leaving a porous surface perfect for absorbing glaze.
  3. Glazing — Dip, pour, or brush liquid glaze onto the bisqueware. Keep the foot ring clean — glaze melts and will stick to kiln shelves.
  4. Glaze Firing — The final, higher-temperature firing (cone 6 or cone 10) vitrifies the clay and melts the glaze into a glassy finish.

Glaze Tips for Thrown Pieces

  • Dipping gives the most even glaze coverage on thrown forms.
  • Wax the foot ring before glazing to keep it clean easily.
  • Inside and outside can have different glazes — they often interact beautifully where they meet.
  • Thick walls from early throwing may need longer drying to prevent cracking.
  • Test glazes on small thrown cylinders before committing to a finished piece.
  • Cone 6 stoneware glazes are the most accessible for beginners.

How to Throw a Vase

A classic vase — wide belly, narrowed neck, flared rim — is one of the most satisfying forms to throw. It builds on cylinder technique and introduces collaring, one of the key skills for any enclosed or necked form.

Wedged clay ball

Step 1

Wedge & Prepare Your Clay

Take 1.5 lbs of stoneware and wedge it thoroughly on a flat surface for 2–3 minutes using the ram's head method. Push the clay forward and down with the heel of your hand, rotate a quarter turn, and repeat. You're removing air bubbles and making the consistency uniform throughout.

Tip: Slice the clay ball in half with your wire cutter. If you see air holes or uneven texture, keep wedging. Smooth cross-section = ready to throw.

Centering on the wheel

Step 2

Center the Clay

Slam the clay firmly onto the center of the wheel head (or a bat). Set the wheel to medium-fast speed. Wet your hands and the clay, then brace your elbows against your thighs. Use the heel of your left hand to push inward from the side while your right hand pushes down from the top. Work the clay into a smooth, wobble-free cone or dome shape.

Tip: The clay should feel like it's "not there" — no vibration, no wobble. Rest one finger lightly against the clay while it spins; if your finger bounces, it's not centered yet.

Opening the floor

Step 3

Open the Clay

With the wheel spinning at medium speed, place both thumbs together at the very center of the clay top. Press straight down slowly and steadily, stopping about ½ inch from the wheel head — that's your floor. Then pull your fingers outward toward you to widen the base to roughly the diameter you want the inside of your vase bottom to be.

Tip: Use your fingertips to compress the floor in circular motions. A compressed floor prevents cracking during drying and firing.

Pulling up the walls

Step 4

Pull Up the Walls into a Cylinder

Place the fingers of one hand inside the wall and the fingers of your other hand on the outside. Pinch gently and pull upward in one smooth, continuous motion from the base to the rim. Add a little water each pull. Repeat 3–4 times, each time thinning the walls and gaining height. Aim for a tall, even cylinder — this is the foundation for your vase shape.

Tip: Let the inside hand lead slightly. Always pull from the bottom — starting mid-wall creates a thick base and thin top. Keep the wheel at medium speed.

Shaping the belly

Step 5

Shape the Belly

Now that you have a cylinder, it's time to give your vase its body. With the wheel at slow-medium speed, place your dominant hand inside the cylinder with fingers pointing outward. Use your outside hand to support and guide. Apply gentle outward pressure from inside — starting at the lower third of the vessel — and slowly move upward, pushing the clay out into a wide, round belly. This is called "bellying out."

Tip: Work gradually over 2–3 passes. The top quarter of the wall should remain straight or slightly inward — you'll collar that into the neck next. Don't let the belly get too wide or the walls will collapse.

Collaring the neck

Step 6

Collar the Neck

Collaring is how you narrow the top of a vase into a neck. Slow the wheel down. Wrap both hands around the upper portion of the cylinder — fingers pointing upward — and gently squeeze inward and slightly upward at the same time. Move slowly and evenly around the clay. The neck will narrow and rise. Repeat 2–3 times to achieve the desired neck width.

Tip: Don't try to collar too much at once — the clay will buckle or tear. Each pass narrows the neck a little. A neck about ⅓ the width of the belly looks proportional and elegant.

Flaring the rim

Step 7

Open & Flare the Rim

With a finger inside the neck and a finger supporting the outside, gently push outward and slightly downward to open and flare the rim into a gentle trumpet shape. Use very light pressure — the neck walls are thin. A soft, flared rim gives the vase a finished, elegant look and softens the overall silhouette.

Tip: Wet your fingers before working the rim. Use a chamois or damp sponge to smooth and compress the very edge of the rim — this prevents cracking as it dries.

Wire off the wheel

Step 8

Wire Off & Let Dry to Leather Hard

Stop the wheel completely. Hold your wire cutter taut and flat against the wheel head, then pull it straight toward you in one smooth motion to cut the vase free. Slide it onto a wooden bat or board and set it in a cool, draft-free spot to dry slowly. It needs to reach "leather hard" — firm enough to handle but not fully dry — before trimming.

Tip: Cover the vase loosely with plastic to slow the drying. This is especially important for a vase with a narrow neck, which dries faster than the belly and can crack if rushed.

Trimming the foot ring

Step 9

Trim the Foot Ring

Once leather hard, center the vase upside down on the wheel. Secure it with three small coils of soft clay (or use a chuck — a cylinder of clay — to hold it upright by the neck). With the wheel spinning at medium speed, hold a loop trimming tool against the base to carve a foot ring — a raised circular rim on the bottom. This lifts the vase slightly, removes excess clay, and gives the piece a refined, finished base.

Tip: Tap the inverted vase gently — it should sound solid, not hollow or dull. A hollow sound means it's too dry to trim well; a very dull thud means it's still too wet and will distort.

Finished & glazed vase

Step 10

Bisque Fire, Glaze & Final Fire

Allow the vase to dry completely — this can take 1–2 weeks depending on thickness and humidity. Then bisque fire it to around 1800°F. Once cooled, wax the foot ring and apply your chosen glaze by dipping the vase into a bucket of glaze for 3–4 seconds for an even coat. Touch up any thin spots with a brush. Leave the foot ring clean, then load it into the kiln for the final glaze firing. Unload after cooling and admire your finished vase.

Tip: A teal or celadon glaze beautifully shows off the curves of a vase form. Try dipping in two different glazes — one for the outside and one brushed inside — for a layered, professional look.

Ready to Sit at the Wheel?

Here's your path from total beginner to confident thrower.

1

Find a Studio with Wheels

Look for community pottery studios or ceramic arts centers that offer wheel throwing classes and open studio access. Most provide all tools and materials.

2

Take a Wheel Throwing Class

A 6–8 week beginner course covers centering, opening, pulling, and basic shaping. In-person instruction is invaluable — hands-on correction makes all the difference.

3

Throw Cylinders Obsessively

The cylinder is the foundation of all thrown forms. Before making bowls or vases, throw 50 cylinders. Centering and pulling up walls becomes muscle memory.

4

Embrace the Collapses

Every thrower has wobbles, collapses, and off-center disasters. Recycle the clay and throw again. Failure at the wheel is just practice in disguise.
