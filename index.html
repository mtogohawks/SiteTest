What is Pottery?

Pottery is one of humanity's oldest crafts — the art of shaping clay into functional and decorative objects. From ancient bowls discovered in archaeological sites to contemporary sculptural art, pottery has evolved over thousands of years while staying rooted in the simple, meditative act of working with earth and water.

Whether you want to make everyday mugs, decorative vases, or abstract sculpture, pottery offers a rewarding creative outlet that connects you to a timeless tradition.

Why Learn Pottery?

  • Reduce stress and practice mindfulness
  • Create functional art you can use daily
  • Connect with a 25,000-year-old tradition
  • Build patience and fine motor skills
  • Join a vibrant creative community

Tools & Materials

You don't need much to start — just clay, a few tools, and some curiosity.

●

Clay

The most common types for beginners are earthenware (soft, great for hand-building), stoneware (durable, versatile), and porcelain (fine, translucent, trickier to work with).

●

Pottery Wheel

Used for wheel throwing, the wheel spins clay so you can pull it into symmetrical shapes. Electric wheels are most common in studios; kick wheels use foot power.

●

Hand Tools

A basic starter kit includes a wire clay cutter, wooden rib, sponge, needle tool, and loop trimming tool.

●

Kiln

A kiln fires clay at high temperatures to harden it. Most beginners use a community studio kiln. Electric kilns are common; gas kilns produce unique atmospheric effects.

●

Glazes

Glazes are glass-like coatings applied before the final firing. They add color, texture, and a waterproof surface. They come in hundreds of colors and finishes.

●

Workspace

A sturdy table, a canvas or plaster bat to work on, good lighting, and easy access to water are all you need for a functional home or studio setup.

Core Techniques

Every potter starts with these foundational methods.

01

Hand Building — Pinch Pots

The simplest starting point. Push your thumb into the center of a ball of clay and pinch the walls outward to form a bowl or vessel. Perfect for beginners — no wheel required.

02

Hand Building — Coil Method

Roll clay into long ropes (coils) and stack them to build up the walls of a piece. Smooth the coils together inside and out. Great for large, irregular, or sculptural forms.

03

Hand Building — Slab Construction

Roll clay flat like a pie crust, then cut and join the slabs to build boxes, tiles, and geometric forms. Excellent for mugs with flat walls or architectural pieces.

04

Wheel Throwing

Center a ball of clay on a spinning wheel, then use your hands to open, pull, and shape the walls upward. The most iconic pottery technique — and one of the most satisfying to master.

05

Trimming & Finishing

Once a piece has partially dried to "leather hard," use trimming tools to refine the shape, foot ring, and surface. This is where much of the final form is defined.

Firing & Glazing

The Firing Process

  1. Greenware — Freshly shaped, unfired clay. Fragile and must dry completely before firing.
  2. Bisque Firing — The first kiln firing (~1800°F / 982°C). Burns off water and organic material, leaving a porous, durable piece ready for glazing.
  3. Glazing — Apply liquid glaze by dipping, pouring, or brushing. Let dry completely.
  4. Glaze Firing — The second, higher-temperature firing melts the glaze into glass, vitrifies the clay body, and produces the final color and finish.

Glaze Tips for Beginners

  • Always glaze bisqueware, not greenware.
  • Keep glaze off the bottom — it will stick to kiln shelves.
  • Test new glazes on sample tiles before applying to a finished piece.
  • Colors shift dramatically during firing — learn your glazes over time.
  • Layering two glazes creates unique effects.
  • Thicker application generally means richer color.

Ready to Get Your Hands Dirty?

Here's how to take your first steps into the world of pottery.

1

Find a Local Studio

Community pottery studios offer open studio time and beginner classes with access to wheels, kilns, and guidance from experienced potters.

2

Take a Beginner Class

A 6–8 week intro course will teach you the fundamentals of hand-building or wheel throwing and get you comfortable with clay.

3

Practice Consistently

Pottery rewards repetition. Even 2–3 hours a week of consistent practice will build muscle memory and intuition rapidly.

4

Embrace Imperfection

Your first pieces will be lopsided and uneven — that's the tradition. Every great potter started exactly where you are now.
