How to Throw a Vase

A classic vase — wide belly, narrowed neck, flared rim — is one of the most satisfying forms to throw. It builds on cylinder technique and introduces collaring, one of the key skills for any enclosed or necked form.

Wedged clay ball

Step 1

Wedge & Prepare Your Clay

Take 1.5 lbs of stoneware and wedge it thoroughly on a flat surface for 2–3 minutes using the ram's head method. Push the clay forward and down with the heel of your hand, rotate a quarter turn, and repeat. You're removing air bubbles and making the consistency uniform throughout.

Tip: Slice the clay ball in half with your wire cutter. If you see air holes or uneven texture, keep wedging. Smooth cross-section = ready to throw.

Centering on the wheel

Step 2

Center the Clay

Slam the clay firmly onto the center of the wheel head (or a bat). Set the wheel to medium-fast speed. Wet your hands and the clay, then brace your elbows against your thighs. Use the heel of your left hand to push inward from the side while your right hand pushes down from the top. Work the clay into a smooth, wobble-free cone or dome shape.

Tip: The clay should feel like it's "not there" — no vibration, no wobble. Rest one finger lightly against the clay while it spins; if your finger bounces, it's not centered yet.

Opening the floor

Step 3

Open the Clay

With the wheel spinning at medium speed, place both thumbs together at the very center of the clay top. Press straight down slowly and steadily, stopping about ½ inch from the wheel head — that's your floor. Then pull your fingers outward toward you to widen the base to roughly the diameter you want the inside of your vase bottom to be.

Tip: Use your fingertips to compress the floor in circular motions. A compressed floor prevents cracking during drying and firing.

Pulling up the walls

Step 4

Pull Up the Walls into a Cylinder

Place the fingers of one hand inside the wall and the fingers of your other hand on the outside. Pinch gently and pull upward in one smooth, continuous motion from the base to the rim. Add a little water each pull. Repeat 3–4 times, each time thinning the walls and gaining height. Aim for a tall, even cylinder — this is the foundation for your vase shape.

Tip: Let the inside hand lead slightly. Always pull from the bottom — starting mid-wall creates a thick base and thin top. Keep the wheel at medium speed.

Shaping the belly

Step 5

Shape the Belly

Now that you have a cylinder, it's time to give your vase its body. With the wheel at slow-medium speed, place your dominant hand inside the cylinder with fingers pointing outward. Use your outside hand to support and guide. Apply gentle outward pressure from inside — starting at the lower third of the vessel — and slowly move upward, pushing the clay out into a wide, round belly. This is called "bellying out."

Tip: Work gradually over 2–3 passes. The top quarter of the wall should remain straight or slightly inward — you'll collar that into the neck next. Don't let the belly get too wide or the walls will collapse.

Collaring the neck

Step 6

Collar the Neck

Collaring is how you narrow the top of a vase into a neck. Slow the wheel down. Wrap both hands around the upper portion of the cylinder — fingers pointing upward — and gently squeeze inward and slightly upward at the same time. Move slowly and evenly around the clay. The neck will narrow and rise. Repeat 2–3 times to achieve the desired neck width.

Tip: Don't try to collar too much at once — the clay will buckle or tear. Each pass narrows the neck a little. A neck about ⅓ the width of the belly looks proportional and elegant.

Flaring the rim

Step 7

Open & Flare the Rim

With a finger inside the neck and a finger supporting the outside, gently push outward and slightly downward to open and flare the rim into a gentle trumpet shape. Use very light pressure — the neck walls are thin. A soft, flared rim gives the vase a finished, elegant look and softens the overall silhouette.

Tip: Wet your fingers before working the rim. Use a chamois or damp sponge to smooth and compress the very edge of the rim — this prevents cracking as it dries.

Wire off the wheel

Step 8

Wire Off & Let Dry to Leather Hard

Stop the wheel completely. Hold your wire cutter taut and flat against the wheel head, then pull it straight toward you in one smooth motion to cut the vase free. Slide it onto a wooden bat or board and set it in a cool, draft-free spot to dry slowly. It needs to reach "leather hard" — firm enough to handle but not fully dry — before trimming.

Tip: Cover the vase loosely with plastic to slow the drying. This is especially important for a vase with a narrow neck, which dries faster than the belly and can crack if rushed.

Trimming the foot ring

Step 9

Trim the Foot Ring

Once leather hard, center the vase upside down on the wheel. Secure it with three small coils of soft clay (or use a chuck — a cylinder of clay — to hold it upright by the neck). With the wheel spinning at medium speed, hold a loop trimming tool against the base to carve a foot ring — a raised circular rim on the bottom. This lifts the vase slightly, removes excess clay, and gives the piece a refined, finished base.

Tip: Tap the inverted vase gently — it should sound solid, not hollow or dull. A hollow sound means it's too dry to trim well; a very dull thud means it's still too wet and will distort.

Finished & glazed vase

Step 10

Bisque Fire, Glaze & Final Fire

Allow the vase to dry completely — this can take 1–2 weeks depending on thickness and humidity. Then bisque fire it to around 1800°F. Once cooled, wax the foot ring and apply your chosen glaze by dipping the vase into a bucket of glaze for 3–4 seconds for an even coat. Touch up any thin spots with a brush. Leave the foot ring clean, then load it into the kiln for the final glaze firing. Unload after cooling and admire your finished vase.

Tip: A teal or celadon glaze beautifully shows off the curves of a vase form. Try dipping in two different glazes — one for the outside and one brushed inside — for a layered, professional look.
